Risk Assessment

Based on your personal history of breast cancer at age 55 and your family history of breast cancer in a maternal aunt diagnosed before age 50, your clinical presentation meets National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) criteria for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) genetic testing. Specifically, the following NCCN criteria apply:

  • Personal history of breast cancer diagnosed at age ≤50, or
  • Personal history of breast cancer at any age with a first- or second-degree relative with breast cancer diagnosed at age ≤50.

Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome is most commonly caused by pathogenic variants in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, which account for approximately 5–10% of all breast cancers. Other moderate- and high-penetrance genes associated with hereditary breast cancer include PALB2, CHEK2, ATM, TP53, PTEN, CDH1, and STK11.

Identification of a pathogenic variant has important implications for:

  • Your ongoing cancer surveillance and management, including consideration of risk-reducing strategies;
  • Your family members, who may be at increased risk and eligible for predictive testing;
  • Potential eligibility for targeted therapies such as PARP inhibitors.

Genetic Testing Recommendation

After discussion of the benefits, limitations, and potential risks of genetic testing, you elected to proceed with multi-gene panel testing for hereditary cancer syndromes. The recommended panel includes analysis of the following genes:

Informed Consent Discussion

We reviewed the elements of informed consent for genetic testing, including:

  • Possible Results: Testing may identify a pathogenic variant (positive), no pathogenic variant (negative), or a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). A VUS is a change in the gene for which there is currently insufficient evidence to classify it as harmful or benign.
  • Implications of Results: A positive result confirms hereditary cancer predisposition and informs medical management and family testing. A negative result does not eliminate all hereditary risk but significantly lowers the probability. A VUS does not change medical management.
  • Insurance and Discrimination: The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) provides federal protection against genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment. GINA does not apply to life, disability, or long-term care insurance.
  • Psychosocial Impact: Genetic test results can have emotional and psychological implications for you and your family. Our team is available to provide support and counseling.
  • Privacy and Confidentiality: Your genetic information is protected under HIPAA. Results will be disclosed only to individuals you authorize.

You expressed understanding of these points and provided written informed consent for testing.
